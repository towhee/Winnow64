Curves

What it does

The tone curve is the map from the tone a pixel has to the tone it ends up with. The panel shows it two ways, chosen by the mode dropdown at the top:

  • Parametric — four draggable bands (blacks, shadows, highlights, whites) that are a picture of the Basic panel's tone sliders.
  • Point — a free-form curve you place points on yourself, for the composite RGB and for each channel separately.

Parametric mode

Drag a region of the curve up or down and the matching Basic slider moves with it: the parametric bands do not have values of their own. That is deliberate — there is one set of tone numbers in the recipe, and two ways to reach them. Drag the shadows band and Basic's Shadows slider follows; type into that slider and the band follows back.

Under the plot are the tone split handles, sharing the plot's x-axis. They decide where blacks end and shadows begin, where the midpoint sits, and where highlights give way to whites. Widen the shadow region and the Shadows slider then covers more of the image. These are the same three handles that sit under the histogram scope — the scopes strip can be hidden entirely, and under the parametric curve is where they belong anyway.

Point mode

Click the curve to add a point and drag to move it; double-click a point to delete it, or double-click the background to reset the channel you are editing. The curve through the points is a monotone interpolation (Fritsch – Carlson), which is what stops a steep S from overshooting into a wiggle: tones that were lighter than their neighbours stay lighter.

The channel dropdown beside the mode picks what you are drawing:

  • RGB is the composite, applied to all three channels — the contrast curve.
  • Red, Green and Blue layer on top of the RGB curve. A channel curve is a colour tool: lifting the blue channel's black point is the classic faded-shadow look; pulling red down in the highlights cools them.

All four curves and the parametric values are folded into one lookup table per channel before rendering, so a heavy curve costs the same as a light one.

The two modes together

Parametric and Point both contribute — switching the dropdown changes which one you are editing, not which one applies. In practice: shape the broad tonality parametrically (it is hard to make an ugly curve that way), then use Point for anything parametric cannot say, such as a flat-toe fade or a per-channel cast.

Working with the panel

  • Double-click in Point mode removes a point (or, on the background, resets that channel's curve); in Parametric mode it resets the band you clicked in, exactly as double-clicking its Basic slider would.
  • The section's [⋮] menu has Show Curves changes (a before / after that keeps your values) and Reset Curves.
  • The header reads Curves * when anything in the panel is off its default.
  • Which mode and channel you are looking at is not part of the recipe — it is where the panel happens to be pointing, and does not travel with a preset.

Where it sits in the pipeline

The tone curve runs after exposure and white balance, on the tone-mapped image, and before the colour work that depends on it. Because the parametric bands and Basic's tone sliders are the same numbers, there is no question of which of the two "wins".

What is saved

The point curves and the tone splits are part of the develop recipe, stored per scope in the sidecar, so a curve can be global or confined to a mask. They travel with presets, Copy / Paste Settings, History and multi-image editing.
