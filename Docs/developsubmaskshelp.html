Submasks

What it does

A mask scope selects part of the image, and it is built out of submasks: a gradient, a brush stroke, a colour range, an AI selection. Each one is a shape or a selection in its own right, and the submask list under the Mask band is where they are combined into the one mask the scope applies.

The terms are worth keeping straight: the mask is what the scope applies; each submask is a building block folded into it, in list order.

The tools

  • Linear Gradient and Radial Gradient — drag on the image to place them; both can be moved, rotated and re-shaped afterwards.
  • Beam Gradient — a band across the photo, strongest along its centre line and fading out the same way on both sides. Drag the centre to move it, an end handle to turn it, and a side handle to set its width. Its Taper slider narrows the beam toward one end (positive) or the other (negative); at 100 the edges meet in a point at that end's handle, like a shaft of light.
  • Brush — paint the selection by hand; Opt/Alt erases.
  • Color Range and Luminance Range — content-based: sample a colour or set a band of tones and the selection follows the picture, not a shape.
  • Subject, Sky, Background, Depth Range and Object — AI selections. Subject / Sky / Background need nothing but a click; Depth Range selects a band of distance; Object asks you to trace the boundary of the thing you want and refines the result to its edge.
  • Segmenter — AI too, but point and click: it finds the objects in the photo (a few seconds; the progress shows at the bottom of the image), highlights each one as the pointer passes over it, and a click adds it to the submask. Click a selected object again to take it back out. Detail chooses what a click picks: whole Objects, their Parts, or finer Details — [ gives smaller pieces and ] bigger ones while the pointer is over the photo. The submask is the union of everything selected.

Adding one

[+] on the Mask band (or M, or Add submask in the band's [⋮] menu) opens the Add submask dialog, which asks the two questions together — what the submask does to the mask, and what kind of submask it is.

  • + Add: the new area joins the mask.
  • - Subtract: the new area is cut out of it.
  • ∩ Intersect: only where the new area and the mask overlap survives.

The first submask in an empty mask has nothing to combine with, so only Add is offered there. Below that, pick the type: Drawn (gradients and the brush), Content (colour and luminance ranges) or Automatic (subject, sky, background, depth, object). Double-click a type to add it in one gesture.

Then define the submask on the picture. There is nothing to press afterwards: two seconds after your last edit it folds itself into the mask and the chip top-left of the photo stops saying what it is about to do. Until then it is pending — Esc discards it, Return lands it straight away and closes its settings. The overlay previews the outcome throughout, so you see the resulting mask rather than having to imagine it.

The old keyboard shortcut still works if you prefer it: hold Opt/Alt while you paint or drag to override the chosen op with Subtract, Shift+Opt/Alt for Intersect. The override sticks once you shape with it held, and letting the key go returns to the op you picked in the dialog. Once the submask has landed its op is changed on its own row instead, and changing your mind about the whole thing is [⋮] → Delete on the row (or Cmd+Z / the History panel).

The list

Each row shows the tool, its combine op and whether it is inverted. Click the row to re-open that submask for editing, and click the open row again to close its settings and put its handles away. A submask that has not landed yet lands first — closing it is you saying you are done with it. Then:

  • The op button left of the eye (+, − or ∩) shows what the submask does to the mask above it; click it to cycle Add → Subtract → Intersect. The first submask has none — it starts the mask, so it always adds.
  • The eye shows or hides that submask's contribution — a one-click A / B of what it is actually doing to the mask. Show all submasks / Hide all submasks in the Mask band's [⋮] menu do the same for all of them.
  • The [⋮] menu holds Edit, the op (Add / Subtract / Intersect, offered from the second row down), Invert, Move up / Move down, Duplicate and Delete.

Order matters. Submasks fold together from the top down, so subtracting before you add gives a different mask than adding then subtracting. If a result surprises you, walk the list with the row eyes.

A row that reads (null) — Subject Submask (null), Sky Submask (null) — is an automatic submask that found nothing in this picture: no sky in an indoor shot, no clear subject, or (for Background) a subject filling the whole frame. It selects nothing, so it changes nothing; delete it, or use a brush or gradient instead.

Feather, Edge, Halo and Invert

The selected submask's own settings appear below the list — Feather, Edge, Invert, and whatever else the tool needs (size for a brush, the sampled colours for a Color Range). Feather softens that submask's edge, and every mask in Winnow uses the same calibrated falloff, so a feather of 50 means the same thing whichever tool drew it. Invert flips that one submask, which is often quicker than describing the opposite: select the sky, invert, and you have everything but the sky.

On a Linear Gradient, the two outer handles mark where the ramp starts and ends, and they are tied to Feather. Raise Feather and the handles spread apart; drag a handle outward and Feather rises with it. At Feather 0 the handles close in on the centre for a hard edge, and at 100 the ramp spans the whole photo.

A low Feather puts those handles close together, which makes them hard to turn, so a Linear Gradient also has a Rotation slider. It turns the gradient about its centre, in degrees clockwise, and follows along when you turn it with the handles.

Edge does what Feather cannot: it moves the boundary instead of softening it. Positive values grow the submask, negative values shrink it, and one unit is one pixel of the full-size image — so the same setting means the same thing on screen and on export.

It is most useful on the AI masks: when Select Subject lands a pixel or two inside your subject and leaves a pale halo of background, an Edge of +2 pushes the cutout out past it. On a Color or Luminance Range, a small negative Edge is a quick way to drop the speckle that any content-based selection picks up, and a small positive one closes pinholes.

At the top of the panel, in the Mask section above the submask list, there is a second Edge belonging to the mask as a whole. It grows or shrinks the finished mask — every submask folded together — so you can choke or spread the assembled result without visiting each submask in turn.

The two add up: a submask at +5 inside a mask at −3 ends up 2 pixels out. The section appears as soon as the mask has a submask to work on; its [:] menu puts its Feather, Edge and Halo back to zero, leaving each submask's own Feather and Edge alone.

Feather, at the top of that Mask section, softens the finished mask as a whole — every submask folded together — so a mask built from several pieces can be softened once instead of submask by submask.

It adds to each submask's own Feather, and it is applied last, after Edge and Halo, so what you set is the softness you see. At 100 the transition spreads across roughly a tenth of the photo's shorter side.

Halo, another row in that Mask section, is for the case Edge cannot fix. When a mask boundary does not quite follow your subject, whatever you do to that mask spills onto a thin rim of background and reads as a glow around the subject — most obvious when you have brightened the subject. Edge only slides the whole boundary by the same amount everywhere, so if the mask is out by different amounts in different places it just moves the problem.

Halo instead finds the edges in the picture near the mask boundary and fades the adjustment out across them, so the rim stops reading as an outline. Raise it until the rim disappears. It does nothing until it reaches far enough to find the real edge, so if nothing seems to be happening, keep going — and if you push it hard, a narrow band just inside your subject can end up slightly under-adjusted, which is the trade.

A mask made only of Linear, Radial or Beam Gradients has no edge in the picture to work from, and there the control is greyed out.

Opacity, under Halo, sets how strongly the whole mask applies, in percent. At 100 the mask's adjustments land at full strength wherever it reaches; at 50 they land at half, everywhere at once, without changing the shape of the mask. It is the quick way to tone a mask down after you have set its sliders. It also fades the mask's kaleidoscope and, with Hide global image on, how solidly the masked area shows. The Mask band's Reset mask Feather, Edge, Halo and Opacity puts it back to 100.

Seeing the mask

The overlay appears by itself while the mask has your attention — whenever the cursor is in this panel, or the last thing you did was a mask edit (painting or dragging a handle on the photo counts, so moving out there to work does not chase it away). Touch an adjustment instead — Exposure, a curve, Transform — and it gets out of the way so you can see the edit. Press O at any point to override it either way; the override lasts until your attention moves again.

With a submask open, the overlay also shows which part of the mask is that submask, using three densities of the one tint colour plus a white outline:

  • Normal — mask, but not the open submask.
  • Denser — where the open submask contributes to the mask.
  • Faint — the open submask where it falls outside the mask.
  • White outline — the open submask's edge, drawn over all of it.

The outline is what finds a Subtract submask: it takes area away, so it contributes nothing to the mask and has no denser region at all — it is entirely faint, inside its outline. On a Colour or Luminance Range submask, whose edges are scattered over the whole frame rather than being a boundary, the outline is left off and the densities tell the story on their own.

The swatch at the right-hand end of the Mask band is the veil's switch: click it to show or hide the overlay (the same as O), and right-click it to pick the tint colour or turn on the grayscale backdrop. Those two describe the veil as a whole rather than any one submask, which is why they sit on the mask's own band and not on a submask's row.

What is saved

The submask list — each tool, its op, its inverted and enabled flags, its feather and its geometry or samples — is part of the develop recipe, stored with the scope in the image's sidecar, as are the mask's own Feather, Edge and Halo. Masks are per image and never travel with a preset, Copy / Paste Settings, or a multi-image edit: they describe these pixels.
