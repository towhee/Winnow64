Submasks

What it does

A mask scope selects part of the image, and it is built out of submasks: a gradient, a brush stroke, a colour range, an AI selection. Each one is a shape or a selection in its own right, and the Submasks list is where they are combined into the one mask the scope applies.

The terms are worth keeping straight: the mask is what the scope applies; each submask is a building block folded into it, in list order.

The tools

  • Linear Gradient and Radial Gradient — drag on the image to place them; both can be moved, rotated and re-shaped afterwards.
  • Brush — paint the selection by hand; Opt/Alt erases.
  • Color Range and Luminance Range — content-based: sample a colour or set a band of tones and the selection follows the picture, not a shape.
  • Subject, Sky, Background, Depth Range and Object — AI selections. Subject / Sky / Background need nothing but a click; Depth Range selects a band of distance; Object asks you to trace the boundary of the thing you want and refines the result to its edge.

Adding one

[+] on the band (or M, or Add submask in the band's [⋮] menu) offers the tool list. The new submask is pending: it exists, and you can see it, but it is discarded if you press Esc or leave the image. Define it on the picture, then commit it with the button below the settings — and the button's label is where the combine happens:

  • no modifier — Add and Commit: the new area joins the mask.
  • Opt/Alt — Subtract and Commit: the new area is cut out of it.
  • Shift+Opt/Alt — Intersect and Commit: only where the two overlap survives.

Hold the modifier before you commit and the overlay previews the outcome, so you see the resulting mask rather than having to imagine it. The first submask in an empty mask has nothing to combine with, so the modifiers are inert there. Return commits as well. Re-open an existing submask from the list and the button just reads Done: its edits are already real, and its op is changed on its own row instead.

The list

Each row shows the tool, its combine op and whether it is inverted. Click the row to re-open that submask for editing, and click the open row again to close its settings and put its handles away. A submask you are still building cannot be closed that way — click its row and Winnow says so — because its commit button would go with it: commit it with Return or discard it with Esc first. Then:

  • The eye shows or hides that submask's contribution — a one-click A / B of what it is actually doing to the mask. The band eye does the same for all of them.
  • The [⋮] menu holds Edit, the op (Add / Subtract / Intersect, offered from the second row down), Invert, Move up / Move down, Duplicate and Delete.

Order matters. Submasks fold together from the top down, so subtracting before you add gives a different mask than adding then subtracting. If a result surprises you, walk the list with the row eyes.

Feather, Edge, Halo and Invert

The selected submask's own settings appear below the list — Feather, Edge, Invert, and whatever else the tool needs (size for a brush, the sampled colours for a Color Range). Feather softens that submask's edge, and every mask in Winnow uses the same calibrated falloff, so a feather of 50 means the same thing whichever tool drew it. Invert flips that one submask, which is often quicker than describing the opposite: select the sky, invert, and you have everything but the sky.

Edge does what Feather cannot: it moves the boundary instead of softening it. Positive values grow the submask, negative values shrink it, and one unit is one pixel of the full-size image — so the same setting means the same thing on screen and on export. It is most useful on the AI masks: when Select Subject lands a pixel or two inside your subject and leaves a pale halo of background, an Edge of +2 pushes the cutout out past it. On a Color or Luminance Range, a small negative Edge is a quick way to drop the speckle that any content-based selection picks up, and a small positive one closes pinholes.

At the top of the panel, in the Mask section above the Submasks row, there is a second Edge belonging to the mask as a whole. It grows or shrinks the finished mask — every submask folded together — so you can choke or spread the assembled result without visiting each submask in turn. The two add up: a submask at +5 inside a mask at −3 ends up 2 pixels out. The section appears as soon as the mask has a submask to work on, and stays put when you collapse the Submasks list; its [:] menu puts both of its sliders back to zero, leaving each submask's own Edge alone.

Halo, the other row in that Mask section, is for the case Edge cannot fix. When a mask boundary does not quite follow your subject, whatever you do to that mask spills onto a thin rim of background and reads as a glow around the subject — most obvious when you have brightened the subject. Edge only slides the whole boundary by the same amount everywhere, so if the mask is out by different amounts in different places it just moves the problem. Halo instead finds the edges in the picture near the mask boundary and fades the adjustment out across them, so the rim stops reading as an outline. Raise it until the rim disappears. It does nothing until it reaches far enough to find the real edge, so if nothing seems to be happening, keep going — and if you push it hard, a narrow band just inside your subject can end up slightly under-adjusted, which is the trade. A mask made only of Linear or Radial Gradients has no edge in the picture to work from, and there the control is greyed out.

Seeing the mask

Press O to show or hide the mask overlay on the image. The tint colour and the grayscale backdrop are set from the Develop action row's tint button (right-click it) — they describe the veil, not any one submask, which is why they are not in this panel.

What is saved

The submask list — each tool, its op, its inverted and enabled flags, its feather and its geometry or samples — is part of the develop recipe, stored with the scope in the image's sidecar, as are the mask's own Edge and Halo. Masks are per image and never travel with a preset, Copy / Paste Settings, or a multi-image edit: they describe these pixels.
