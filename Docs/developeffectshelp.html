Effects

What it does

Effects holds two finishing controls that work on the rendered image, after demosaic and after the tone and colour work: a vignette and film grain. Like every other section it is per scope, so either can be confined to a mask.

Noise reduction used to live here. It now sits in the Detail panel alongside Sharpening, because the two are one decision — noise reduction removes exactly the detail sharpening puts back.

Choosing an effect

The list at the top of the panel picks the effect to work on, and only that effect's sliders are shown below it. Choose Vignette, Grain, Blur, Orton or Patterns.

Every effect is always applied, whichever one is showing: the list only chooses which sliders you see. An effect marked * in the list has been changed, so you can tell from the list that grain is on while you are looking at the vignette. Winnow remembers the last effect you chose.

The eye and reset at the right end of the list's row act on the effect that is showing, and nothing else. The eye hides that one effect without losing its settings, so you can compare with and without it; the reset puts its sliders back to their defaults (for Patterns, that turns it off). The eye on the Effects header still hides all of them at once.

Vignette

  • Amount is the corner exposure in stops (EV), ±5.00: negative darkens the corners (the familiar look), positive brightens them, which is how you correct a lens that falls off.
  • Feather is how far the falloff reaches in toward the centre. Low values keep it in the corners; high values make it a broad, soft dome.

The vignette is radial about the image centre. For an off-centre or shaped darkening — around a subject that is not in the middle — use a radial gradient mask and put an exposure adjustment in that scope instead.

Grain

Monochromatic grain added to luminance, which is what film grain actually is. Size and Roughness are sub-controls of Amount and do nothing until Amount is above zero.

  • Amount — the strength of the grain.
  • Size — particle size, fine to coarse. It is scaled to the image, so what you see on the editing proxy is what you get at full resolution.
  • Roughness — how patchy and irregular the grain is. Low is an even sprinkle; high clumps it, which reads as a faster, older stock.

Grain is also a practical tool, not only a look: a little of it hides banding in smooth skies and disguises the plastic texture that heavy noise reduction leaves behind.

Patterns

Mirrors the picture around a centre point, the way a kaleidoscope does: the frame is divided into wedges, and each one shows the same slice of the photo, every other wedge reflected so the seams always meet as mirror images.

On the Global scope it mirrors the whole picture. On a mask scope it mirrors only what the mask covers: everything outside the mask is treated as transparent, and the mirrored result is laid over the photo. Mask a flower, and the flower is repeated around the centre over the unchanged picture. Only masked content inside the slice being mirrored shows up, so if nothing appears, turn Source or Angle, or move the centre, until the slice takes in your subject. Each scope has its own pattern, and a mask scope's adjustments come with it: brighten the flower in that scope and the reflections are bright too.

Type picks the kind of pattern. Mirror is the wedges described here. Hyperbolic tiles a disk with polygons that get smaller and smaller toward its rim without ever reaching it, as in M. C. Escher's Circle Limit prints. Polar wraps the whole photo round the centre, a “tiny planet”. Droste puts the photo inside itself, again and again, and twists the copies into a spiral, as in Escher's Print Gallery. Wallpaper tiles a slice of the photo across the whole frame in one of the 17 ways a flat pattern can repeat. Fluted glass shows the photo through reeded glass, the ribbed kind used in shower doors and shop fronts.

  • Segments (Mirror) is the number of wedges, in even numbers so every seam is a mirror (an odd setting moves up to the next even one). 0 is off, and 2 is a plain mirror across one line.
  • Sides (Hyperbolic) is the number of sides of each polygon: 3 for triangles, 4 for squares, and so on. 0 is off. The middle polygon is exactly a Mirror pattern with twice as many segments; every polygon around it is its mirror image.
  • Per corner (Hyperbolic) is how many polygons meet at each corner. Fewer sides need more polygons at a corner for the pattern to curve: triangles need at least 7, squares 5, and anything larger 4. If you go below that, it moves back up.
  • Disk (Hyperbolic) is the size of the disk, in percent of half the picture's short side, so 100 just fits a centred disk in the frame. Outside the disk the pattern is mirrored across the rim, so the frame has no empty corners. Above 100 the rim moves out of the frame.
  • Copies (Polar) is how many times the photo goes round the centre. 0 is off. The bottom of the photo is at the centre and the top on the planet's edge (the dashed circle); Zoom sets the planet's size.
  • Panorama wrap (Polar) wraps the photo round once per copy as it is. Use it for a 360 degree panorama whose two ends meet. Off, each copy is the photo followed by its mirror image, so there is no seam whatever the photo.
  • Inside out (Polar) puts the top of the photo at the centre instead: a tunnel instead of a planet.
  • Strands (Droste) is how many arms the spiral has. 0 is off.
  • Inner size (Droste) is the size of each copy of the photo inside the last, in percent. The edges of the copies are part of the look: crop first if the photo has a border or something frame-like, and the copies sit inside it.
  • Reverse (Droste) turns the spiral the other way. Zoom slides along the spiral.
  • Pattern (Wallpaper) picks how the tiles repeat: plain repeats, half turns, mirrors, glides (a mirror image shifted along), and square, triangle and hexagon kaleidoscopes. Patterns with mirrors join without seams; patterns built only from turns and glides show where the copies of the photo meet, which is part of their look.
  • Tile (Wallpaper) is the size of each tile, in percent of the picture's short side. 0 is off. Zoom magnifies the slice inside each tile.
  • Width (Fluted glass) is the width of each reed, in percent of the picture's short side. 0 is off. The reeds stand upright until you turn them: the yellow spoke points across them.
  • Refraction (Fluted glass) is how strongly each reed bends the picture behind it. 0 is flat glass. Higher values squeeze more of the picture into each reed; below 0 each reed shows its strip reversed.
  • Shading (Fluted glass) adds light and shadow across each reed, as if lit from the side, so the glass reads as rounded.
  • Angle turns the mirror lines.
  • Source turns the slice of the photo that gets mirrored without turning the pattern, so the design stays put while its content changes.
  • Zoom magnifies the mirrored slice about the centre, in percent: 200 shows the middle of the slice twice as large, so a small detail near the centre fills the pattern. Below 100 takes in more of the photo, reaching further out from the centre.
  • Opacity blends the pattern with the photo underneath: 100 is the full pattern, lower values let the unmirrored picture show through. On a mask, it fades the mirrored copies laid over the photo.

While Patterns is the effect showing and it is on for the selected scope, its handles are on the image: drag the white centre, or click anywhere on the image to move the centre there, and drag the yellow spoke to turn the mirror lines (hold Shift to snap to 15 degree steps). The faint dashed lines are the seams between the wedges; with Hyperbolic, a dashed circle marks the disk's rim, and with Polar the planet's edge. Choose another effect, or fold the Effects panel away, and the handles go — the pattern itself stays. Hold Space to pan the image while they are up.

Patterns works on the cropped picture, so the crop decides what gets mirrored: crop to a detail first. While the Transform panel is open, and while a mask or the spot brush is on the image, the picture is shown un-mirrored so what you draw lands where you see it.

Blur

The artistic blurs: soft focus, movement and glow, applied on purpose for the look. A lens blur, which imitates a wide aperture by blurring with depth, belongs to the Lens panel and is separate from these.

Pick one with Type; only the sliders that type uses are shown. Each scope has its own blur, so on a mask it blurs just the masked part — blur the background to make a subject stand out, or put Motion on a car and nothing else.

  • Gaussian — an even soft blur. Amount is how soft.
  • Motion — a streak in one direction, like panning or a moving subject. Angle sets the direction; on the image, drag the yellow spoke.
  • Spin — a rotation blur around a centre, like a spinning wheel. Drag the white centre on the image, or click where it should be.
  • Zoom — streaks radiating out from a centre, like zooming the lens during the exposure. The centre stays sharp.
  • Tilt-shift — a sharp band with blur coming in above and below it, which makes a scene look like a miniature. Angle turns the band, Size is its width and Feather how gradually the blur arrives. On the image the solid lines are the band's edges and the dashed lines where the blur is full.
  • Glow — a soft bloom laid over the photo: light spills into the darker areas around it while the detail underneath stays. Size is how far the glow spreads. It only ever brightens, so highlights stay crisp. For a soft-focus, dreamy look, use Orton instead.

The square at the top of Blur is the same 1:1 view the Detail panel has: a live 100% look at part of the image. Click its target button and then the photo to choose the part, or drag inside the square. The point is shared with the Detail panel.

The blur is worked out on the full picture, so it looks the same on the small preview, on the loupe and in an export. Grain is added after it, so grain stays crisp on top of a blur.

Orton

The Orton effect, after the photographer Michael Orton, who sandwiched a sharp slide with an out-of-focus copy of the same scene. Winnow lays a blurred copy of the picture back over it: the sharp picture shows through a soft, glowing one.

  • Opacity — how strongly the soft copy shows. 0 is off; 100 is the soft copy alone. Most pictures want 20 to 50.
  • Size — how soft the copy is, the radius of its blur.
  • Brightness — brightens the soft copy before it is laid over the photo (in stops, up to 2). Orton's original slides were over-exposed, which is what gives the look its light, airy glow. At 0 the picture keeps its overall brightness.

Orton or Glow? Both lay a blurred copy over the photo; they differ in how it is laid on. Glow (a Blur type) only ever brightens: light spreads into the shadows, but bright detail stays sharp and nothing gets darker. Orton blends the copy in, so it softens everything, highlights included: small bright details spread and dim, dark areas lift, and the whole picture takes on a soft-focus look. Orton is its own effect, not a Blur type, so it can be combined with any blur, including Glow.

Like the other effects, Orton is per scope: on a mask it softens just the masked part.

Working with the sliders

  • Double-click a slider returns it to its default (Feather 50, Size 25, Roughness 50, both Amounts 0; for Orton, Size 50 and Opacity and Brightness 0).
  • Click a slider and the arrow keys nudge one unit, Page Up / Page Down ten.
  • The section's [⋮] menu has Show Effects changes (a before / after that keeps your values) and Reset Effects.
  • Reset Effects turns Patterns off too, and the eye hides it.
  • The header reads Effects * when anything in the panel is off its default, and the list marks which effect it is.

Where it sits in the pipeline

Both run late, on the rendered image: the vignette, then sharpening (in the Detail panel), then grain last of all — grain belongs on top of the finished picture, not underneath a vignette that would modulate it, and not under a sharpener that would crisp it up.

Orton and Blur run after sharpening and before grain, Orton first, so a blur blurs the softened picture and grain stays crisp on top of both.

Patterns runs after all of that, and after the crop, straighten and perspective in the Transform panel: it folds the finished, cropped picture.

What is saved

Every value is part of the develop recipe, stored in the sidecar, and travels with presets, Copy / Paste Settings, History and multi-image editing. Every effect is stored per scope, so each mask can have its own.
