Basic

What it does

Basic is the panel almost every edit starts in: the white balance the image is rendered at, the tone of it — exposure, contrast and the four tonal regions — and two presence controls, Texture and Dehaze. Get Basic right and the rest of the module is refinement.

White balance

Temp and Tint are absolute, not relative nudges. Temp is a real Kelvin value (2000–50000) on a logarithmic track, because a linear one would bury every useful daylight temperature in the first sixth of the slider. A higher Kelvin says the light was bluer, so the picture renders warmer. Tint runs green to magenta, ±150.

Temp = 0 is a state of its own: as shot, the temperature the camera recorded. The WB row above the sliders carries the two ways in:

  • The dropper (or the W key). It stays armed until you click a pixel, press it again, or press Esc. Click something that ought to be neutral grey — not a specular highlight, which carries no colour information. Opt/Alt-click a lit area of skin instead and Winnow solves for plausible skin rather than for neutral.
  • The preset dropdown: As Shot and Auto on any file, plus the named illuminants — Daylight, Cloudy, Shade, Tungsten, Fluorescent, Flash — on raw files only, since a display-referred file has no camera-neutral reference for them to mean anything against. Custom is what the dropper and the sliders leave you in. Double-click the row to go back to As Shot.

Tone mapping

Tone mapping chooses how the huge brightness range a raw file records is squeezed into the much smaller range a screen or a print can show. Every photograph needs this step — the choice is only ever which one.

It sits at the head of the tone controls because that is the relationship: it sets the overall mapping, and the sliders below it — Exposure, Contrast, Highlights and the rest — adjust within that mapping. The same Exposure value does not mean quite the same thing under each setting, which is why it is worth choosing first.

  • Filmic is the default and the look Winnow has always had: a brightness lift and a smooth highlight roll-off. Familiar and contrasty.
  • Soft roll-off rolls highlights off far more gradually, and — the real difference — a bright saturated colour fades toward white as it brightens instead of changing hue. Under Filmic a saturated red pushed hard drifts toward orange, and a blue toward purple, because the channels run out one at a time. Reach for it on sunsets, stage and neon lighting, fire, and anything with a bright saturated subject. It looks flatter and less punchy at first glance; that is the trade for keeping the colour. (This is the transform published as AgX, if you have read about it elsewhere.)
  • None applies no mapping at all. The image will look very dark and flat. It is there for checking what the sensor actually recorded, not for finishing a picture.

The setting applies to the whole image, not to a mask, so it stays the same whichever scope you are editing. On JPEGs and other non-raw files it is greyed out and reads raw only: those already carry the tone mapping their camera applied, and doing it twice would wreck them.

Changing Tone mapping changes what the Basic sliders feel like — AgX has much more room in the highlights before anything clips — so an image set up under one is worth a second look under the other.

Tone

  • Exposure is in stops (EV), ±5.00, applied in linear light — it is the same operation as having exposed the frame differently.
  • Contrast pivots the image about mid grey.
  • Highlights and Shadows recover or open up the two ends without moving the extremes: they are the gentle controls.
  • Whites and Blacks set the end points themselves — where the image clips. These are the blunt ones, and the histogram scope is the place to watch them.

The four tonal sliders act on regions of the tone range, and where those regions begin and end is adjustable: the handles under the histogram scope (and under the parametric curve in the Curves panel) split the range into blacks | shadows | highlights | whites. Move a split and the same slider value covers more or less of the image.

The Curves panel's Parametric mode is another face of these same four sliders — drag a band of the curve and the slider here moves with it. There is no hidden second copy of the values.

Texture, Clarity and Dehaze

These three are the same kind of control — local contrast — at three very different scales. That is the whole trick to using them: they act on different sizes of detail, so they stack instead of fighting, and reaching for the right one is a question of how big the thing you want to change is.

  • Texture — the finest scale. Positive crisps up skin pores, bark and fabric; negative smooths them while leaving edges and overall contrast alone.
  • Clarity — about five times wider. This is midtone punch: it makes a landscape read as more three-dimensional without touching fine detail. Negative gives a soft glow, which flatters portraits and mist.
  • Dehaze — the widest. It removes (or adds) atmospheric veiling: positive pulls contrast and colour back out of a hazy distance, negative lays haze on, which is a quick way to soften a background or fake distance.

Clarity is not just a wider Texture. Two things hold it back where a wide local-contrast boost normally goes wrong. It is weighted toward the midtones, so it will not crush your blacks or flatten your highlights the way a heavy-handed clarity does elsewhere; and it eases off at strong edges, which is where a control at this scale would otherwise leave a bright halo — the giveaway rim around a tree against a bright sky. You can push it further than you would expect before it looks processed.

None of the three is a sharpener. They change contrast at a detail scale, not edge acutance. For acutance — recovering the edge definition demosaic costs a raw file — use Sharpening in the Detail panel, which works about sixty times finer than Clarity. Using all four together is normal.

Strong positive Dehaze deepens shadows, so it often wants a little Shadows back afterwards. Clarity does not, which is frequently the reason to reach for it instead.

Working with the sliders

  • Double-click a slider returns it to its default.
  • Click a slider and the arrow keys nudge one unit, Page Up / Page Down ten.
  • The section's [⋮] menu has Show Basic changes — a before / after that keeps your values — and Reset Basic.
  • The header reads Basic * whenever anything in it is off its default, so an edited panel is obvious while collapsed.

Where it sits in the pipeline

White balance is applied at the front, in linear light, before demosaic-time work is finished. Exposure follows in linear light; the tone sliders and the tone curve are one combined curve applied after that; Texture and Dehaze run on the rendered image. Because white balance and exposure are upstream, changing them changes what every later adjustment is looking at — which is why they are worth settling first.

What is saved

Every Basic value is part of the develop recipe, stored per scope in the sidecar, so Basic can be set globally or confined to a mask. The values travel with presets, Copy / Paste Settings, History and multi-image editing like anything else.
